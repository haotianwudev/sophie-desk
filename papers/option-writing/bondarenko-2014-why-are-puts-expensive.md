# Why Are Put Options So Expensive?

- **Authors:** Oleg Bondarenko
- **Year:** 2014 (Quarterly Journal of Finance 4(3), 1450015; working paper 2003)
- **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1142/S2010139214500153](https://doi.org/10.1142/S2010139214500153)
- **PDF:** `bondarenko-2014-why-are-puts-expensive.pdf` — the open 2003 working-paper draft

## Testable Hypothesis

The high abnormal returns generated by selling out-of-the-money S&P 500 put options represent a statistical arbitrage that cannot be rationalized by standard equilibrium asset-pricing models, even after accounting for peso problems, unobserved jump regimes, or generalized state-dependent utility functions.

## Summary

Applies a non-parametric, model-free statistical arbitrage testing methodology to historical S&P 500 index option prices from 1987 to 2000. Shows that simple mechanical strategies of selling monthly at-the-money and out-of-the-money put options generate average annualized excess returns exceeding 40% with high Sharpe ratios. Proves that this "put anomaly" violates no-arbitrage bounds for any path-independent pricing kernel and cannot be attributed to sample selection bias (the Peso problem) or rational risk premia within standard expected utility or jump-diffusion frameworks.

## Detailed Summary

### 1. Methodology & Model-Free Rationality Restriction

Bondarenko formulates a non-parametric, model-free statistical arbitrage testing framework to resolve the classic joint-hypothesis problem inherent in tests of the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH).

1. **The Martingale Pricing Restriction Deflated by RND**:
   - In standard EMH tests, security price $Z_t$ with single terminal payoff $Z_T$ at time $T$ must satisfy $\mathbb{E}_t [Z_s m_s] = Z_t m_t$ for $t < s < T$. Testing this requires pre-committing to a specific parametric pricing kernel $m_t$.
   - Bondarenko (2003a) proves that under general conditions, securities prices deflated by the conditional risk-neutral density (RND) $h_t(v)$ evaluated at the realized terminal price $v_T = v$ follow a martingale:
     $$\mathbb{E}_t^v \left[ \frac{Z_s}{h_s(v)} \right] = \frac{Z_t}{h_t(v)}, \quad t < s < T$$
     where $\mathbb{E}_t^v[\cdot] \equiv \mathbb{E}_t[\cdot \mid \tilde{v}_T = v]$ denotes the expectation conditional on the terminal price being $v$.
   - Defining the weight $\lambda^v := h_t(v)/h_s(v)$ (the inverse "return" of the RND evaluated at the final price $v$), and applying the condition to the risk-free bond ($Z_t \equiv 1$), we obtain $\mathbb{E}_t^v[\lambda^v] = 1$ and the moment condition for net returns $r_i = Z_s/Z_t - 1$:
     $$\mathbb{E}_t^v [\lambda^v r_i] = 0 \implies \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}_t^v [\lambda^v r_i] \right] = 0$$

2. **Average Weighted Return (AWR)**:
   - For non-overlapping monthly holding periods $[t_j, s_j] = [T_{j-1}, T_j]$ conditioned on the subsequent expiration date $T_{j+1}$, the empirical test forms the Average Weighted Return:
     $$\text{AWR} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n \lambda^j r^j, \quad \text{where } \lambda^j = \frac{h_{t_j}(v_{T_{j+1}})}{h_{s_j}(v_{T_{j+1}})}$$
   - Although computing $\lambda^j$ uses future realized information ($v_{T_{j+1}}$), this exact look-ahead conditioning construct cancels out unobserved preferences without requiring knowledge of the pricing kernel. Under the null hypothesis of no statistical arbitrage, $\text{AWR}$ must be indistinguishable from zero.

3. **Invariance Properties of the Model-Free Test**:
   - **Preference Independence**: Valid for any path-independent pricing kernel $m_T = m(v_T)$ (or projected kernel $m^*_t(v_T) = m^*_s(v_T)$), including CRRA, habit formation, Epstein-Zin recursive utility, and multi-factor jump-diffusions, regardless of representative investor utility $U(v_T)$.
   - **Robustness to the Peso Problem & Selection Biases**: Because the condition holds state-by-state for every terminal realization $v$, omitting unobserved catastrophic crash states from small historical samples does not distort the expectation ($\bar{I}_3 = 0$).
   - **Robustness to Mistaken Beliefs**: Holds in an Efficiently Learning Market (ELM; Bossaerts, 2003) where investors hold biased priors ($g_0 \ne f_0$) but update rationally via Bayes' rule with correct likelihoods.

### 2. Data & Sample Universe

- **Sample Period**: August 1987 – December 2000 (14 years; 161 monthly rollover cycles; 144 usable periods for the two-month RND evaluation).
- **Underlying Market**: Daily prices of S&P 500 futures options traded on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME), obtained from the Futures Industry Institute. Futures options avoid the 15-minute cash-market closing mismatch of CBOE index options and remove dividend modeling ambiguity.
- **Moneyness Classification**: Options sorted into 7 equally spaced moneyness bins centered at $k = K/v_t \in \{0.94, 0.96, 0.98, 1.00, 1.02, 1.04, 1.06\}$.
- **Data Filtering & Cleaning**:
  - Settlement prices used to eliminate stale quotes and bid-ask bounce.
  - Excluded illiquid options with prices below $0.5\%$ of underlying value ($<\$5$ when $v_t = 1000$).
  - American-to-European price conversion via Barone-Adesi and Whaley (1987) approximation (early-exercise adjustment $< 0.2\%$ of option price).
  - Monotonicity and convexity no-arbitrage shape restrictions enforced via Constrained Convex Regression (CCR; Bondarenko, 2000).
  - RNDs estimated nonparametrically via Positive Convolution Approximation (PCA; Bondarenko, 2000, 2003b) requiring $\ge 8$ strikes per cross-section.

### 3. Key Quantitative Results

#### Historical Put Returns & Unhedged Short Profits (Table 1)
- **Monotonic Negative Returns for Put Buyers**:
  - **ATM Puts ($k=1.00$)**: Average monthly return **-39%** ($n=161$; 95% CI: -54% to -24%; median -100%; max +540%).
  - **2% OTM Puts ($k=0.98$)**: Average monthly return **-54%** ($n=159$; median -100%; max +725%).
  - **4% OTM Puts ($k=0.96$)**: Average monthly return **-58%** ($n=109$; median -100%; max +953%).
  - **6% OTM Puts ($k=0.94$)**: Average monthly return **-95%** ($n=67$; median -100%; max +42%).
  - **ITM Puts**: Mean monthly returns of -26% ($k=1.02$), -17% ($k=1.04$), and -11% ($k=1.06$).
- **Subsample Robustness (Figure 2)**: Average put returns remained significantly negative across all four subperiods (1987–1990: -27% to -12%/mo; 1990–1993: -97% to -22%/mo; 1994–1997: -92% to -14%/mo; 1997–2000: -55% to -12%/mo).
- **Asymmetric Skewness**: Put return distributions show heavy positive skewness for buyers (4.90 for $k=0.94$, 3.85 for $k=0.98$, 2.52 for ATM), reflecting regular complete expirations punctuated by rare extreme spikes.

#### Risk-Adjustment & Failure of Canonical Models (Table 2)
- **CAPM Rejection**:
  - Puts have large negative betas due to negative market correlation and leverage ($\beta = -22.78$ for ATM puts; $\beta = -23.07$ for $k=0.98$; $\beta = -20.42$ for $k=0.96$).
  - Jensen's alpha is negative and significant at the 1% level across strikes: $\alpha = -23\%$ per month for ATM puts, $\alpha = -38\%$ for $k=0.98$, $\alpha = -43\%$ for $k=0.96$, and $\alpha = -94\%$ for $k=0.94$.
- **Leverage-Invariant Metrics**: Sharpe ratios for selling ATM and OTM puts substantially exceed the market benchmark (S&P 500 futures SR = +0.18/mo; put buyer SR = -0.35 for ATM, -0.40 for $k=0.98$, -3.93 for $k=0.94$). Treynor's measure and Modigliani $M^2$ confirm extreme overpricing.
- **Rubinstein (1976) Power Utility Failure**:
  - Calibrating $m(r_m) \propto (1+r_m)^{-\gamma}$ reveals that no single relative risk aversion $\gamma$ fits across moneyness. While deep ITM puts ($k=1.06$) match the market $\gamma = 4.3$, required risk aversion explodes to $\gamma = 8.3$ for ATM, $\gamma = 9.7$ for $k=0.98$, and $\gamma = 130.9$ for $k=0.94$.

#### Extreme Return Analysis & Peso Problem Calibration (Tables 3 & 4)
- **Timing of Peak Losses (Table 3)**: The October 1987 crash period (10/16–11/20/1987, underlying -14%) was only the 4th worst month for put sellers because ATM IV was already high (28%). The largest losses occurred when unexpected sell-offs hit during calm, low-IV environments: August 1990 (Kuwait invasion, ATM put +540%, IV 14%), August 1998 (Russian default / LTCM, ATM put +403%, IV 14%), and September/October 1987 (pre-crash month, ATM put +388%, IV 19%).
- **Crashes Required to Break Even (Table 4)**: To bring the average ATM put return to zero ($AR = 0$), **18 additional October 1987 crashes** (+346% return) would need to be added to the 14-year sample, equivalent to **1.29 crashes per year** (one crash every 9.3 months). For $k=0.98$, 20 additional crashes (1.43/year) are required.

#### Bull Market / Trend Explanation Test (Table 5)
- Over 1987–2000, the S&P 500 rose $>4\times$ from 314.59 to 1312.15.
- To explain put returns through underlying drift alone, a negative drift $\eta = -1.5\%$ per month (-18%/year) would be required for ATM puts to break even, which would force the final S&P 500 index level down from 1312.15 to 111.9. For $k=0.98$ and $k=0.96$, required negative drifts are -2.0% and -2.7% per month (final index levels of 47.8 and 13.3).

#### Economic Magnitude & Wealth Transfer (Section 2.3.4)
- Cumulative net dollar P&L transferred from put buyers to put sellers across CME S&P 500 futures options totaled **$17.8 billion** over the 14-year sample ($0.1B in 1987–1990, $2.3B in 1990–1993, $6.5B in 1994–1997, $8.9B in 1997–2000).

#### Rejection of the Model-Free Rationality Restriction (Table 6 & Section 3.3)
- Average Weighted Return (AWR) on 1-month puts strongly rejects the martingale restriction across all strikes:
  - ATM ($k=1.00$): $\text{AWR} = -0.35$ ($p < 0.01$; 95% CI: -0.53 to -0.12).
  - 2% OTM ($k=0.98$): $\text{AWR} = -0.51$ ($p < 0.01$).
  - 4% OTM ($k=0.96$): $\text{AWR} = -0.59$ ($p < 0.01$).
  - 6% OTM ($k=0.94$): $\text{AWR} = -0.79$ ($p < 0.01$).
- Two-month rollover puts also reject the restriction: $\text{AWR} = -0.22$ for ATM ($p < 0.01$) and $-0.33$ for $k=0.96$ ($p < 0.05$).
- In contrast, the S&P 500 futures contract satisfies the restriction ($\text{AWR} = +0.50\%$, $t = 1.09$, insignificant).
- **Core Conclusion**: The put anomaly is an intertemporal pricing inconsistency (statistical arbitrage) that cannot be rationalized by any path-independent equilibrium model, even after allowing for crash/Peso problems and mistaken beliefs.

#### Implications for the "Pricing Kernel Puzzle" (Section 4.3)
- Directly challenges empirical literature extracting risk aversion $\gamma(v_T)$ from $h_t(v_T)/f_t(v_T)$ that claims investors are locally risk-seeking ($\gamma < 0$; Jackwerth, 2000; Aït-Sahalia and Lo, 2000).
- Demonstrates that because the true pricing kernel is path-dependent, static projections $m^*_t(v_T)$ generate spurious non-monotonicities; the "pricing kernel puzzle" is an artifact of assuming path independence ($m_T = m(v_T)$) rather than evidence of non-concave utility.

### 4. Relevance to Option Research

In `sophie-option-research`, Bondarenko (2014) provides the definitive theoretical and empirical justification for systematic index option writing:
1. **Structural Alpha of Systematic Put Selling**: Empirically proves that selling ATM and OTM S&P 500 puts generates annualized excess returns exceeding +40% with massive CAPM alphas (+23%/month for ATM), establishing that the returns harvested in `lab/strategy.py`, `notebooks/03_baseline_backtests.ipynb`, and `notebooks/04_param_sweep.ipynb` reflect a structural, non-arbitrageable volatility risk premium.
2. **Definitive Refutation of the Peso Problem**: Demonstrates via formal model-free bounds and crash calibrations that the put premium is not an artifact of missing rare disasters (requiring 1.3 October 1987 crashes per year to break even), validating long-term premium capture strategies against claims of unobserved sample bias.
3. **Regime-Dependent Volatility Shock Defense**: Shows that peak seller losses occur during sudden drops following low-IV regimes (e.g., Aug 1990 Kuwait invasion and Aug 1998 Russian default) rather than during post-crash high-IV states. This directly informs the VRP regime-filtering and dynamic sizing mechanics in `notebooks/09_vrp_study.ipynb` and `notebooks/11_cross_asset_vrp.ipynb`.
